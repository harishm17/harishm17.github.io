The chemistry score measured markup: 65.9% was right while the score said 10.8%

ReadableSTEM scored near zero on chemistry. Checking every equation showed most were right as chemistry. The score had compared how the answer key spells a formula.

Personal project, · 2026, · 4 min read

Updated October 23, 2026

My part. Every analysis and decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, MathML

Summary

Claim
The registered score said 10.8% of chemistry equations were right. As chemistry, 65.9% were. The score compares the shape of the MathML, and the answer keys spell one formula in several shapes.
Sample
443 chemistry equations in 12 development documents (79 pages), then 377 equations in five fresh OpenStax Chemistry sections, registered before the run.
Caveat
I tuned the chemistry work on the development documents, and the chemistry score is one I wrote after seeing the problem. On the fresh sections the figure was 56.0%, not 65.9%.

On the registered textbook test, ReadableSTEM scored 0.04 on chemistry sections, and so did the chatbots, against 0.61 on Precalculus (the results). That looked like a failure to read chemistry. It was mostly something else.

What the score compared

The primary score compares the shape of the MathML. OpenStax writes a formula such as H2O(l) with a text run for each group of element symbols and the state symbol as three tokens. My converter writes one upright letter for each symbol. A reading that says the same chemistry in a third shape is wrong on the primary. What I counted

I put each of the 443 equations in one class, from the recorded answers, with no model call. In 244 of them the formula was right and the primary score was wrong only because of how the key spells it: how tokens are packed (75), how scripts are grouped (39), and upright against italic against text letters, bars, numbers and the length of an arrow (130). Counting those, 292 of 443 were right as chemistry. The primary score counted 48.

What was really missing

With the score corrected, the problem was detection, not reading. When ReadableSTEM found an equation and matched it to the key, it was right as chemistry in 292 of 319. What it missed were the 94 formulas in table cells, which were off in that run as they are in production, and the lone × signs, which are not chemistry at all.

The fresh check

I didn’t change the registered score. I added a second one for chemistry only and report both. Then I wrote down predictions and ran the five fresh sections once, with no change afterwards. The primary was 0.135, which I had predicted would fall between 0.05 and 0.20. The chemistry score was 0.552 before the chemistry fixes and 0.560 after, 4.1 times the primary, against a prediction of at least 3.

What this doesn’t show

  • The development numbers come from documents I tuned on. The fresh sections gave 56.0%, and the first edition and the Atoms First edition share text, so I chose topics the development set lacks and did not measure the overlap.
  • The chemistry score is mine, written after the problem was visible. To check it isn’t too generous, 15 pairs of near formulas must stay wrong in its tests, and on the authored document all 100 equations it calls right and the primary calls wrong have the same LaTeX once spacing, text commands and the arrow command are removed.
  • It doesn’t put ReadableSTEM ahead of the chatbots. Scored the same way on five development sections, the Haiku chatbot got 49.3% right as chemistry and ReadableSTEM 38.2%, where the chatbot found more formulas in table cells (it missed 42, ReadableSTEM 94). I did not run the chatbots on the fresh sections.

Lessons for your own evals

  1. A score can measure markup rather than meaning. Before you treat a low score as a failure, put a sample of the “wrong” answers in classes by hand.
  2. Don’t move the registered score. Add a labelled second score, report both, and say which one the verdict used.
  3. Test the forgiving score for generosity. Write the near misses it must still call wrong, and check it against cases where you know the answer.
  4. Fix the instrument first. Only then did the real problem, detection, show.
  5. Check the key. Three of the twelve equations I read by hand were glitches in the key, not in the reading.
