ReadableSTEM led a chatbot on figures and heading levels, trailed on equations

Registered tests against a chatbot given one sentence of instruction, and against three open parsers. It trailed the chatbots on equations, and one claim I registered was not met.

Personal project, · 2026, · 10 min read

Updated October 23, 2026

My part. Design, evaluation protocol, review and every decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, PyMuPDF, Claude API, MathML

Summary

Claim
ReadableSTEM led a chatbot on heading levels and figure descriptions, and trailed the Opus chatbot on equations on all three registered sets. Two registered bars were missed: the claim that it clearly beats the chatbot on equations, and the floors for finding tables.
Sample
15 OpenStax sections (242 pages, 1,369 equations), then two sets of 15 arXiv papers, each fixed before any system ran on it. Handwriting is 24 pages twice, and tables are 7 papers.
Caveat
With 15 sections or papers a set, the intervals are wide. On the textbooks the registered rule called none of its five comparisons in ReadableSTEM’s favour, and the figure judge is a Claude Opus model that I haven’t checked against people.

ReadableSTEM turns STEM course PDFs into accessible web pages. This page reports how it did on tests I registered before any system ran on them, including the ones it didn’t pass. The causes of its loss on equations are in a separate write-up.

How the tests were run

I wanted a test I couldn’t tune to, so I wrote down the sections, the metrics and the comparisons before any system ran on them. The textbook set is 15 sections from five OpenStax textbooks (242 pages, 1,369 equations, 509 headings), picked by a seeded rule that excludes every section I used in development. OpenStax publishes these books as web pages with expert-written MathML and alt text, so each section comes with an answer key. I printed each section to a PDF, ran it through the real pipeline and scored the output against the key. The configuration was frozen on the development sample first, and nothing was tuned on these sections.

I compared it with a general chatbot given Purdue’s one-sentence prompt for PDF to accessible HTML (Claude Opus and Claude Haiku, two runs each) and with three open parsers: Docling, MinerU at its basic tier, and PyMuPDF4LLM. Every model call, ReadableSTEM’s included, went to Claude, so against the Opus chatbot the test is my pipeline against a one-sentence prompt on the same model. A few things changed after registration, including two comparisons I didn’t run; PROOF.md lists each one. The model answers are committed, so make proof replays the whole report with no key and no network.

For papers I used the same method on arXiv: 15 papers under open licences (71 pages, 1,826 equations), scored against the authors’ own LaTeX, which is closer to a real course PDF than a printed OpenStax page. Later I registered a second set of 15 papers for the fixes described in the other write-up.

What I registered, and how each test came out

The rule for the textbook set was that a system could be called better only when the 99% interval of the paired difference excluded zero. That interval is wider than the usual 95%, because there were five comparisons with the chatbot. Otherwise the wording is “no difference this set can detect”.

Where it led

Heading levels and figure descriptions were not among the five registered comparisons, so I report them as descriptive. Heading F1 was, and the registered rule called it no difference this set can detect.

  • Headings. Its F1 lead over the chatbots is too small for 15 sections to tell from noise. Among the headings both find, it also gets the level right more often (1.000 against 0.341 and 0.508), which is table stakes for a tool that exists to restore an outline.
  • Figures. By the judge, 0 of 138 ReadableSTEM descriptions state something the image contradicts, against 3 of 134 for the Opus chatbot and 12 of 133 for Haiku. The judge is Claude Opus, which hasn’t been checked against people and is also ReadableSTEM’s main model.
  • Cost. The Haiku chatbot is the hard baseline: about an eighth of the cost per page, and 0.600 of equations right. What the extra cost buys is heading levels and figure descriptions.
  • Open parsers. They write no figure descriptions and cost nothing in API calls. On equations ReadableSTEM is well ahead of MinerU, the only one of the three I ran that writes LaTeX (0.504 against 0.174). MinerU’s heading F1 is 0.923, and PyMuPDF4LLM gets every matched level right but finds fewer headings (F1 0.830).

Where it trailed

  • Equations end to end. The Opus chatbot finds about as many equations (88.7% against 88.9%) but gets more of the ones it finds exactly right (0.70 against 0.57). With 15 sections the gap is likely, not certain. The scorer is strict too: hand-written LaTeX known to be correct scores 0.70 on a 40-equation sample, so every number reads low.
  • Added symbols. In 8.7% of the equations it writes, it adds a symbol the PDF doesn’t have, against 2% for the Opus chatbot. The chatbot, though, writes many more equations the PDF has no match for (29% against 3%).
  • Chemistry. Equations there are near zero for every system: 0.04 for ReadableSTEM, against 0.61 on Precalculus. Most of that turned out to be the score rather than the reading, which a separate piece explains.
  • Review catches too little. 23.3 of every 100 equations are wrong and nobody is asked about them, about the same as the chatbot, which asks nothing. The equation flags catch 0.34 of the real errors, and the heading flags catch none.
  • Review takes time. It asks for 1.76 decisions per page, against 0.23 on the development sections: about 53 decisions for a 30-page upload.

On real papers the ordering held. On 15 held-out arXiv papers ReadableSTEM got 0.583 of the equations exactly right and both chatbots 0.641, a gap too small for 15 papers to separate. One paper the Opus chatbot refused counts as a zero, as registered; without it the gap is larger. ReadableSTEM cost $0.030 a page, against $0.083 for the Opus chatbot and $0.002 for Haiku. The claim that was not met

I traced that gap to its causes, fixed the ones that carried it, and tested on 15 more papers, again registered before any system ran. The fixes took it from 0.558 to 0.626 of equations right, level with the Haiku chatbot (0.622) and still behind Opus (0.682). The claim I had registered, that it clearly beats the chatbot recipe, was not met. The causes and the fixes are in the other write-up.

Silent errors

A silent error is a wrong equation that nobody is asked about. A chatbot asks nothing, so all of its wrong equations are silent. ReadableSTEM asks, which is why the number can be lower. On the textbooks it was about the same as the chatbots. On papers it was lower by point estimate, and on the second set the paired difference was clearly lower: 7.9 per 100 below Haiku and 5.2 below Opus.

Handwriting is where the count surprised me. My first handwriting run had no silent wrong equations, and a fresh set had 2 of 192, both in blocks the model called high confidence. One was not a wrong reading at all, only a different notation, and the score still counts it:

Handwriting and scans

The handwriting set is 24 pages of real human ink, 205 expressions from Google’s MathWriting set, registered before the run. ReadableSTEM got 0.566 of the equations exactly right (interval 0.515 to 0.618), so 43% were wrong. None was silent, because the model called most handwritten blocks medium or low confidence and so 5.7 decisions a page were required, against 1.4 on typed pages. It also dropped four whole display equations of one page without a flag, which the tool now catches by comparing the page’s ink with what it read. The cost was $0.066 a page. By the letter of my rule, an advertising bar of 0.50 and zero silent errors, both held. They did not on the fresh set: 0.589 right on 24 pages (192 expressions), and 2 of 192 wrong without being flagged. Two things changed: every equation, and every paragraph or list item with a formula in it, on a page read from its image is now a decision for the instructor whatever the model’s confidence, and the omission check. The ceiling is the ink, as the other write-up shows.

Scans of typed pages worked better. Made into scans, 9 of the arXiv papers came out at 0.664 of equations exactly right, against 0.619 for the same papers as typed PDFs, at $0.136 a page.

Tables

In the textbook run tables were off, so a detected table was kept as an image and sent to review. Since then tables are rebuilt as real tables. On a fresh set of seven papers, registered before the run, it found 0.744 of the tables, and 0.914 of what it called a table was one. That missed both floors I had set, 0.85 for recall and 0.95 for precision. I fixed the causes that run showed, so that set is now a development set, and I don’t have a fresh test of the fixes.

What the tests leave out

  • The textbook sections are clean OpenStax pages from retired first editions, printed with my own stylesheet. Real course PDFs are harder.
  • OpenStax is public, so the models may have seen these books. I haven’t tested for that, and every Claude arm shares it.
  • I haven’t checked whether a wrong equation changes the meaning, or whether the figure judge agrees with a person.
  • It’s one recording on Claude, with two runs per task on the textbooks and one on the papers.
  • The textbook results are for pipeline version 0.4.0, the version I registered. The later sets ran on later versions, and I wrote some of those fixes after seeing the development sets’ errors.
  • Heading levels are compared with each system’s own top level, and I haven’t checked how much of the chatbots’ low score is that convention and how much is wrong levels.

Lessons for your own evals

  1. Write down what “better” means before the run. With five comparisons the bar was a 99% interval, and it declared neither side better on the textbooks. The differences I care about most, heading levels and figures, are descriptive because I hadn’t registered them.
  2. Report the cheap baseline. The Haiku chatbot costs about an eighth as much per page and got 0.600 of equations right against 0.504. A comparison with only the expensive chatbot would have hidden it.
  3. Count the errors nobody is asked about, not only the score. On the textbooks both kinds of system left about 23 to 25 wrong equations in every 100 unflagged. On papers, asking cut that to 8.4 against 13.7 and 16.3.
  4. A claim that is not met stays not met. I registered that it would clearly beat the chatbot recipe. It drew level with one chatbot and trailed the other, so I report it as not met, and the table floors the same way.
  5. A zero in a small sample can fail to replicate, and numbers from the data you tuned on run high. No silent errors in 205 handwritten expressions became 2 of 192 on a fresh set, and table recall of 0.920 on development became 0.744 on a fresh set.
