Why ReadableSTEM lost on equations, and what I changed

A chatbot beat it on equations. I traced the gap to its causes before changing anything, then tested the fixes on papers I hadn’t looked at.

Personal project, · 2026, · 7 min read

Updated October 22, 2026

My part. Every analysis, fix and decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, PyMuPDF, Claude API, MathML

The first write-up ends with a loss: a general chatbot got more equations exactly right than ReadableSTEM, on textbooks and on arXiv papers. This one is about what I did next. The rule I set was to find out why from the recorded outputs, which costs nothing, before writing any fix or spending anything on new runs.

Where the points went

I took the 25 papers I developed on and gave every equation that ReadableSTEM or the Haiku chatbot got wrong exactly one cause, the first rule that fit. On these papers ReadableSTEM was 12.4 points behind. Five causes account for almost all of it:

Points of equation score ReadableSTEM lost to the Haiku chatbot, by cause (25 development papers)
CausePoints
Short variables in running text, a lone S or m set in an italic text font wasn’t seen as maths3.9
Where an inline formula starts and ends, it kept the sentence’s parentheses, units and words3.6
Maths inside table cells2.9
Maths inside figure and table captions1.7
Bold italic letters, a bold vector read as plain bold0.6

Reading was not the problem. Among the equations it found, its wrong readings were 6.6% of the answer key, against 9.1% for the chatbot. It lost because it didn’t see some equations, or cut them in the wrong place.

The idea the evidence ruled out

An obvious next step was a repair loop: render the equation the model wrote, compare the picture with the PDF, and retry on a mismatch. Before building it I checked what it could catch. Of the 282 wrong equations nobody is asked about, 1 had the right symbols in the wrong arrangement, which is the only kind of error a render-and-compare check can see. The rest had other symbols, which the text-layer check already looks at, or a span cut at an edge, where the render matches the cut and the cut is wrong. One in 282 was far below the bar I had set for building it, so I didn’t build it.

I also had a model read each whole page as a second witness for finding equations. It raised the score by 1.7 to 2.5 points on the development papers, but it added 0.6 to 1.1 more silent errors per 100 equations, against a limit of 0.5 that I had set. I dropped it and fixed the same causes from the PDF’s text layer instead.

The fixes and the test

The fixes use the text layer and the position of each glyph, with no change to the prompt, so a recorded model answer replays to the same result. An italic letter between upright words is a variable. A figure’s caption is cut into text and formulas like a paragraph. The parentheses of the sentence, a unit after a number, and a citation leave the formula. A bold letter with an index is a vector.

On the development papers the score went from 0.553 to 0.680, level with Haiku at 0.677. That says little, because I tuned on those papers. So I registered a second test first: 15 new arXiv papers, the rules for success written down, and a limit that silent errors could not rise by more than 0.5 per 100. Results:

  • The fixes were worth +0.068 (interval +0.031 to +0.116), from 0.558 to 0.626.
  • Silent errors did not rise: 9.76 per 100 before, 8.42 after.
  • ReadableSTEM is level with Haiku (0.626 against 0.622) and 5.6 points below Opus (0.682, with an interval that includes zero).
  • The claim I registered, that it clearly beats the chatbot recipe, was not met.

What it does have is fewer errors nobody is asked about: 8.4 per 100 equations, against 16.3 and 13.7 for the two chatbots. For a tool whose job is to tell an instructor what to check, that is the number I care about more. The run cost $13.55 of an $18 cap.

Handwriting: the ceiling is the ink

On real handwriting the first test gave 0.566 right, so 43% wrong. I looked at the wrong ones instead of trying prompts. Of 199 wrong equations over three sets, 65% were ink, or a label, that a reader can’t settle from the page. Another 23% were a notation the label hadn’t used, 8% were misreads of clear ink, and 4% were writing the model left out. A model reading each expression alone from clean ink got 61% to 64% right, the same as reading the whole page. So the accuracy is limited by the writing, not by how I ask.

The model’s confidence told me almost nothing either: it separated right equations from wrong ones barely better than chance (area under the ROC curve, 0.52 to 0.61). That settled the design. I stopped trying to raise accuracy and made the errors visible: on a page read from its image, every equation and every block with a formula in it goes to the instructor. I measured the cost by replaying the recorded answers. Required decisions per page went from 3.58 to 6.44 on scanned typed pages and from 2.00 to 5.69 on scanned papers. Silent wrong equations in those replays are 0 by construction.

Two earlier results needed correcting. My first handwriting run had 0 silent wrong equations, and that did not replicate: a fresh registered set of 24 pages had 2 of 192, both in blocks the model called high confidence. And one page had lost four equations without a flag, which is why the tool now compares the page’s ink with the regions it read.

Chemistry: the score measured markup

Chemistry scored about 0.04 for every system, which looked like a failure of reading. On the 443 chemistry equations in the development set, I classified every outcome. 65.9% were right as chemistry, while the score said 10.8%. In 244 cases the formula was right and the score was wrong only because of how the answer key spells it: how tokens are packed, how scripts are grouped, upright against italic letters, the length of an arrow.

I didn’t change the registered score. I added a second score for chemistry only and report both. What was really missing was detection: formulas in table cells (the table step was off in that run) and lone symbols the key counts as equations. I fixed arrows with a label over them and a notation case that made the converter refuse an equation. On five fresh sections of OpenStax Chemistry (377 equations, registered before the run), the registered score is 0.135 and the chemistry-aware one is 0.552 before the fixes and 0.560 after.

Tables

The layout model proposes ruled, shaded or header-marked grids only, so unruled tables and tables ruled only above and below were never proposed. On five real documents the detector found 14 of 25 tables (recall 0.560). Tracing each miss gave a list of mechanisms: the justified columns, a caption added after the decision, a page of sideways text, a page with more than 30,000 drawn items. The fixes took development recall to 0.920, which again says little. On a fresh set of seven papers registered first, it found 0.744 of the tables with precision 0.914, and missed both floors. The misses there were Roman-numeral captions, bulleted cells and charts boxed by the layout model, none of which the development set had shown. I fixed those too, and that set now counts as development.

What this doesn’t show

  • Every cause was found on my own development papers, and every fix was tuned on them. Only the fresh sets are evidence, and each is 15 papers or fewer.
  • The chatbots are one recording each, scored by one scorer. Two notation artefacts count against every system by about a point and a half.
  • Maths in table cells is still the largest cause I haven’t fixed, along with formulas that wrap across lines.
  • The handwriting is touchscreen ink from one dataset, pasted onto composed pages. It is not paper handwriting.
