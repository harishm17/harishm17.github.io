ReadableSTEM turns STEM PDFs into accessible pages

Turns STEM course PDFs into accessible web pages. On 15 textbook sections it had never seen, a general chatbot scored higher on whole-page equations, and ReadableSTEM scored higher on heading levels and figure descriptions.

Personal project, · 2026, · 5 min read

Updated October 14, 2026

My part. Design, evaluation protocol, review and every decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, FastAPI, PyMuPDF, Claude API, MathML, PostgreSQL, Railway

A screen reader can read the words in a course PDF, but an equation is often a picture or a scatter of glyphs that it reads badly or skips. ReadableSTEM takes a STEM course PDF and returns one web page: equations as MathML a screen reader can speak, headings and lists as real HTML, and short alt text for figures. Anything it is unsure about goes to a review screen, where the instructor accepts, edits or rejects it. It assists a person and doesn’t certify a page as accessible.

It matters now because of the Justice Department’s ADA Title II web rule. Under its interim final rule of April 20, 2026, state and local governments with a total population of 50,000 or more have until April 26, 2027 to make their web content meet WCAG 2.1 AA, with some exceptions. The rule may still change.

How it is measured

The test is pre-registered: I wrote down the sections, metrics and comparisons before any system ran on them. It uses 15 sections from five OpenStax textbooks (242 pages, 1,369 equations, 509 headings), picked by a seeded rule that excludes every section I used in development. OpenStax publishes these books as web pages with expert-written MathML and alt text, which makes a ready answer key. Each section is printed to a PDF, run through the real pipeline and scored against that key. I froze the configuration on the development sample first, and nothing was tuned on these sections. It is self-registered: the registration and the answer key’s hashes are in the repo’s own commits, and nothing outside the repo dates them.

I compared it with a general chatbot given Purdue’s one-sentence prompt for PDF to accessible HTML (Claude Opus and Claude Haiku, two runs each) and with three open parsers: Docling, MinerU at its basic tier, and PyMuPDF4LLM. Several things changed after registration, and each is written down in an addendum. The OpenAI account ran out of credit, so every model call, chatbots and figure judge included, went to Claude, and ReadableSTEM ran without its second automated check, which is OpenAI’s. I wrote that change down before any model saw a held-out section. Two registered comparisons were not run: a chatbot with an expert-written prompt, and Docling with formula recognition. ReadableSTEM ran with Claude Opus as its main model and Claude Haiku as its cheap one, so against the Opus chatbot the test is the pipeline against a one-sentence prompt on the same model. The recorded model answers are committed, so make proof replays the whole report at the tagged commit with no key and no network.

The open parsers write no figure descriptions and cost nothing in API calls. On equations ReadableSTEM is well ahead of MinerU at its basic tier (0.504 against 0.174), the only one of the three parsers I ran that writes LaTeX. Docling can with formula recognition on, which I didn’t run. On headings, its F1 lead over the chatbots and MinerU (0.923) is too small for 15 sections to tell from noise, and the clearer gap is levels, where the chatbots get 0.341 and 0.508 right. PyMuPDF4LLM also gets every matched level right but finds fewer headings (F1 0.830). The figure scores come from a Claude Opus judge that hasn’t been checked against people, and Opus is also ReadableSTEM’s main model.

The cheap chatbot is the hard baseline. Claude Haiku with the same one-sentence prompt costs $0.0015 a page, about an eighth of ReadableSTEM, and gets 0.600 of equations right end to end. What the extra cost buys on this test is heading levels and figure descriptions: by the judge, 0 of 138 ReadableSTEM descriptions state something the image contradicts, against 12 of 133 for Haiku.

Where it loses

  • Equations end to end. The Opus chatbot got 0.622 right and ReadableSTEM 0.504. The chatbot finds about as many equations (88.7% against 88.9%) but gets more of the ones it finds exactly right (0.70 against 0.57). Given each equation as a clean image, ReadableSTEM scores 0.684, which isn’t a fair comparison with the chatbot because I didn’t measure the chatbot that way. With 15 sections the gap is likely, not certain. The scorer is strict, too: hand-written LaTeX known to be correct scores 0.70 on a 40-equation sample, so every number reads low.
  • Added symbols. On the equations it writes, it adds a symbol the PDF doesn’t have 8.7% of the time, against 2% for the Opus chatbot. The chatbot writes many more equations the PDF has no match for (29% against 3%).
  • Chemistry. Equations there are near zero for every system, 0.04 for ReadableSTEM against 0.61 on Precalculus. Three sections per book are too few to say why.
  • The review screen misses most wrong equations and every wrong heading. 23.3 of every 100 equations are wrong and nobody is asked about them, about the same as the chatbot, which asks nothing. The equation flags catch 0.34 of the real errors and the heading flags none. I haven’t labelled which wrong equations change the meaning.
  • Review load. It asks for 1.76 decisions per page, against 0.23 on the three development sections. That’s about 53 decisions for a 30-page upload, the most the app takes.

What the test leaves out

  • The sections are clean OpenStax pages from retired first editions, printed with my own stylesheet. Real course PDFs are harder.
  • OpenStax is public, so the models may have seen these books. I haven’t tested for that, and every Claude arm shares it.
  • I haven’t checked whether a wrong equation changes the meaning, or whether the figure judge agrees with a person.
  • It’s one recording on Claude, with two runs per task, and ReadableSTEM’s two document runs differ by 0.007 on equations.

Try it

ReadableSTEM is live at readablestem.com as a free, open-source beta for a small group of instructors, by invitation. These results are for pipeline version 0.4.0. The code has changed since (the repository is at 0.5.0, and the demo was rebuilt on it), so they describe that tagged version, not necessarily what runs today. The demo is one converted OpenStax section and needs no sign-in. PROOF.md has every table and interval (it still uses the tool’s old name, a11y-stem), and the code is on GitHub.
