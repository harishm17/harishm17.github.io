ReadableSTEM turns STEM PDFs into accessible pages

Upload a STEM course PDF or LaTeX source and get back one web page a screen reader can read, equations included. The PDF’s own text checks what the models read, and anything uncertain goes to the instructor.

ReadableSTEM turns a course PDF or LaTeX source into one web page a screen reader can read, and puts every reading it isn’t sure of to the instructor. It is free for anyone with a Google account, the demo (opens in a new tab) needs no sign-in, and how well it works is in the evaluation, from tests I registered (wrote down and hashed) before running them.

What it does

An instructor uploads a course PDF, a LaTeX file or a zip of a LaTeX project and gets back one web page. Equations are in MathML, the web’s markup for math, which a screen reader (software that reads a page aloud) speaks with their structure. Headings and lists are real ones, tables are rebuilt as tables, and each figure has a short description.

Anything the tool isn’t sure of becomes a decision for the instructor on a review screen. The page downloads as HTML, Word or EPUB, or as a Canvas page or Common Cartridge package for a course platform. ReadableSTEM doesn’t certify a page as accessible; the instructor decides.

It exists because a screen reader can read the words in a course PDF but not the math: an equation there is glyphs (character shapes) placed on a page, and read in a row they lose their structure. Under the Justice Department’s interim final rule of April 20, 2026 (opens in a new tab), state and local governments with a total population of 50,000 or more, public universities among them, have until April 26, 2027 to make their web content meet WCAG 2.1 AA, the standard for accessible web content, with some exceptions; the rule may still change.

How it works

ReadableSTEM is one FastAPI service and one Postgres database. The database is the job queue, the file store, the spend ledger and the cache of model answers, which leaves one place to delete a person’s data from. The service runs on Railway for about $5 to $8 a month before model spend.

A worker claims a queued job with SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED and a 120-second lease that it renews while working. Jobs with the fewest attempts go first, then the one whose owner was served longest ago, then the oldest: one person’s ten documents don’t keep another person’s single one waiting. If the worker dies, the lease runs out and the job is retried, up to 3 times. Model answers are cached per element, and a retry pays only for what’s missing.

The output is one self-contained HTML file with no scripts. Every other format is made from it, which leaves one document to review and check. Its MathML is rebuilt from an allowlist of permitted tags, never pasted from a model’s answer.

Reading the page without a model

The layout comes from the PDF itself. PyMuPDF, a PDF parsing library, reads the text layer (the characters, fonts and positions stored in the file) with no model call. That makes the layout free and deterministic, and it gives every later check something independent of the models to compare against.

A row that is at least half math with no prose words becomes a display equation, and tables are found first and taken out, which keeps their numbers from being read as equations.

Two models, and when to escalate

Each equation, figure and table is cropped from the page and read by a vision model: Claude Opus as the main model and Claude Haiku as the cheap one. Short inline equations start on Haiku and escalate to Opus when its confidence is below 0.80, its output is invalid, or a check against the PDF fails.

The text layer says where an inline formula starts and ends in its sentence, and it is often wrong at a radical, an integral sign or a stacked fraction. So the model also sees the whole line with the cut-out formula boxed, and writes out the full formula the box belongs to. On a registered test this raised exactly right equations by 2.6 points (interval +1.7 to +3.6) and silent errors did not rise, which met the rule I had set for turning it on.

Checking each answer against the PDF

The equation model’s confidence ranked its own errors worse than chance, so confidence alone can’t decide what to accept. Each answer is checked against the PDF’s text layer instead. The model is never sent an equation’s own text layer and can’t copy it.

The checks on each equation
CheckWhat it does
Text comparisonThe letters, digits and Greek letters in the reading against the PDF’s, then the numbers (36 is not 63), capitals, bold vectors and signs. Any mismatch needs a decision.
Glyph checksLetter styles read from the fonts (italic against upright, bold against bold italic, script, double-struck), and a reading that runs past its box. Chemistry gets a species check on coefficients, charges, states and arrows.
Glyph reconcileFixes a reading only where the glyphs allow one answer, such as a named lookalike symbol in a PDF made by TeX. The fix is an optional note beside the model’s reading, and the instructor sees both.
Flag policyAdds a decision for an accepted equation that looks like the readings that turned out wrong. A small logistic model with 22 features and fixed constants, fitted on development documents only, with no model call at run time. It can only add decisions.

Most wrong readings differ from the text layer in nothing these comparisons can see, which is why the flag policy exists. With these features its flag recall (the share of wrong equations that get a decision) came in under the bar I had registered, as the evaluation reports.

The instructor decides

Nothing is published on its own. A reading that fails a check, or that the model isn’t sure of, becomes a decision for the instructor, and everything else is accepted and listed. Every decision costs the instructor time, so only problems that change what a student reads are required: on the first textbook run, that rule took required decisions across 43 pages from 65 to 10. With the flag policy on, the newest test set needed 2.87 decisions a page, a price I accepted for fewer silent errors.

The review screen shows the page beside each decision, with both readings where there are two, and one action accepts every equation whose checks passed. “Listen as a student” plays roughly what a screen reader would say. An owner can share a finished document with named people to view or to review: one access check covers every route, and each decision is recorded under the reviewer’s name.

Scans, handwriting, chemistry and figures

A scanned or handwritten page has no text layer to check against. It is converted only when the instructor asks, and everything read from its image goes to review. I chose that after the handwriting tests showed the writing limits accuracy whatever the prompt, as the evaluation reports.

Chemistry is read as MathML shaped so a speech engine pronounces it. Charts and diagrams also get a long description, and charts with readable values get a data table, under rules that stop the model inventing a value.

LaTeX source, in a sandbox

An instructor who writes in LaTeX already has the exact equations, and reading them back from a compiled PDF would throw them away and guess them again. A .tex file or a zip of a project goes instead to LaTeXML, an open-source converter that expands the macros as the author’s TeX would and writes MathML. Its output becomes the same document model a PDF becomes, with the same review and exports.

The catch is that a .tex file is a program. It can read files, start programs and loop forever, and the web service holds the model keys in its environment. LaTeXML therefore runs only inside a sandbox: a read-only root with no application, no network, an empty environment, and limits on memory, CPU time, file size and wall-clock time.

The sandbox is bubblewrap, or Landlock with a seccomp filter where bubblewrap cannot start, as on Railway. It fails closed: if the sandbox cannot start, the job fails with “upload the PDF instead” and nothing runs outside it. A start-up probe has to pass on the deployed image before the feature may be on, and it did on November 4, 2026.

I considered a separate service with no secrets and haven’t built it. The registered results are all for the PDF path, and source jobs have no measured result yet.

Treating every input as untrusted

Every upload is first opened in a child process with a 30-second limit and a 768 MB memory cap, where a crash or a hang kills the child and leaves the server running. Of 837 malformed test files, 41 crashed the parser, and all 41 were stopped there.

A file can pass that probe and still hang or exhaust memory later. The whole conversion also runs in a second child process that the worker can kill, with a 3,072 MB address cap, a CPU limit and the job’s wall-clock limit. That child holds no model keys and no database address: model requests, spend reservations and writes stay in the web process.

Prompt injection is bounded too: the only PDF text a model receives is a short piece of nearby context, fenced as data, the model has no tools, and everything it returns is validated. An injected instruction can at worst produce a wrong equation for review.

Spend capped before it happens

Sign-up is open and I pay the model bill, which means the limit has to hold under concurrency, retries and bugs. Every model call reserves its worst-case cost before it’s sent, then settles at its real cost.

The daily cap ($10) is one row per day, updated with a conditional UPDATE that concurrent calls can’t overshoot. Documents from new accounts may use only $4 of it, which stops strangers from taking the instructors’ whole budget, and a new account gets 150 pages a day. Jobs ($6) and pages have their own caps, stored bytes are capped per person, and the provider’s own limit is the last backstop.

Money runs out before capacity does: at the $10 daily cap and $0.022 a page, the limit is about 450 pages a day. At real scale the worker moves out of the web process into its own service; the queue already lives in Postgres and is already fair between people, so no other part has to change.

Built but off, and what is next

A second model read of each whole page is built and off: its first version raised the score by 1.7 to 2.5 points on development papers but added 0.6 to 1.1 silent errors per 100, over my bar of 0.5, and its second changed no reading (the evaluation has both).

Four more settings are built and off. Display grouping (joining the pieces of one printed display) waits on a registered run, and a second reader for handwriting on a measurement of what it adds. Beamer source and zips of several documents haven’t been tried on real course files, and Word and PowerPoint upload needs a converter the deployed image doesn’t have.

Still open:

  • The registered claim still needs silent errors under 4.0 per 100 and a higher flag recall. That is the next equation work.
  • Chemistry is below its bar of 0.80 right (the chemistry score).
  • The long figure descriptions are unmeasured: the second figure set has a pre-registration that I haven’t signed.
  • The screen-reader test has a written protocol and no results.
  • The accessibility conformance report (the form institutions ask a vendor for) is a draft, and the Canvas export hasn’t been tried in a real Canvas.
