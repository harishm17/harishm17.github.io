ReadableSTEM turns STEM PDFs into accessible pages

Upload a STEM course PDF and get back one web page a screen reader can read, equations included. Anything it isn’t sure about goes to the instructor to decide.

Personal project, · 2026, · 4 min read

Updated October 23, 2026

My part. Design, evaluation protocol, review and every decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, FastAPI, PyMuPDF, Claude API, MathML, PostgreSQL, Railway

ReadableSTEM is live at readablestem.com, free for anyone with a Google account. The demo needs no sign-in, and the code is on GitHub. How well it works is in two other write-ups: the registered results and why it lost on equations.

The problem

A screen reader can read the words in a course PDF but not the maths. In a PDF an equation is a set of glyphs placed on the page: a fraction bar is a drawn line, and a subscript is smaller text set a little lower. The screen reader gets the symbols in a row with the structure gone, or skips them. Figures rarely have descriptions, and nothing marks the headings, so a student can’t jump from section to section.

Universities now have a deadline. Under the Justice Department’s interim final rule of April 20, 2026, state and local governments with a total population of 50,000 or more have until April 26, 2027 to make their web content meet WCAG 2.1 AA, with some exceptions. The rule may still change.

What it does

An instructor uploads a PDF and gets back one web page: equations in MathML, which a screen reader speaks with their structure, real headings and lists, and a short description for each figure. After each conversion it says what changed. This is its summary for the sample document, a 13-page section of OpenStax Physics:

It assists a person and doesn’t certify a page as accessible.

Who it is for

The instructor or accessibility staff member who has to fix a course PDF and can’t recheck every equation by hand, and the student who reads the result with a screen reader. It reads textbook sections and arXiv papers, and pages with no text layer, such as scans and handwriting, when the instructor asks.

Design decisions

PyMuPDF reads the page layout, and Claude reads the equations and describes the figures, with Claude Opus as the main model and Claude Haiku as the cheap one. Four decisions shape the rest.

Nothing is published on its own. The models make mistakes, and an instructor can’t recheck every equation in a course. So a change the tool isn’t sure of becomes a decision for the instructor, and everything else is accepted and listed so they can look it over. The price is the questions: on the held-out textbook sections it asks 1.76 per page, about 53 for a 30-page upload.

Each answer is checked against the PDF, not against the model. Most PDFs carry a text layer, so the symbols, numbers and signs in an equation the model wrote have to match what the PDF contains. A short inline equation starts on the cheap model. If its answer fails a check, or the model isn’t sure, it goes to the main model, and whatever still fails goes to the instructor. I did it this way because a model’s own confidence is a weak signal; on handwriting it was close to a coin flip, as the errors write-up shows.

Spend is capped before it happens. Sign-up is open and I pay the model bill. Every model call reserves its worst-case cost first, and there are caps per page, per job and per day. A job that reaches a cap stops early and says which parts weren’t converted.

A page with no text layer can’t be checked, so a person checks all of it. Scanned and handwritten pages are read from their image only when the instructor asks. With no text to compare against, every equation and table read that way goes to review.

Try it and read the results

The demo converts the sample section with no sign-in. The code and the full results are on GitHub, and make proof replays the whole held-out report from the committed model answers with no key and no network.

I tested it against a general chatbot and three open parsers, on sets I registered before any system ran. The short version: it did better on heading levels and figure descriptions, and a chatbot got more equations exactly right. The registered results report each test, including the claim I registered that was not met. Why it lost on equations traces the gap to its causes. A third, short piece covers the chemistry score, which measured markup more than chemistry.
