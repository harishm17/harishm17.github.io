ReadableSTEM turns STEM PDFs into accessible pages

Upload a STEM course PDF and get back one web page a screen reader can read, equations included. Anything it isn’t sure about goes to the instructor to decide.

Personal project, · 2026, · 5 min read

Updated October 21, 2026

My part. Design, evaluation protocol, review and every decision. It’s a solo project.

Built with. Python, FastAPI, PyMuPDF, Claude API, MathML, PostgreSQL, Railway

ReadableSTEM is live at readablestem.com, free for anyone with a Google account. The demo needs no sign-in, and the code and the full results are on GitHub.

The problem

A screen reader can read the words in a course PDF but not the maths. In a PDF an equation is a set of glyphs placed on the page: a fraction bar is a drawn line, and a subscript is smaller text set a little lower. The screen reader gets the symbols in a row with the structure gone, or skips them. Figures rarely have descriptions, and nothing marks the headings, so a student can’t jump from section to section.

Universities now have a deadline. Under the Justice Department’s interim final rule of April 20, 2026, state and local governments with a total population of 50,000 or more have until April 26, 2027 to make their web content meet WCAG 2.1 AA, with some exceptions. The rule may still change.

What it does

An instructor uploads a PDF and gets back one web page: equations in MathML, which a screen reader speaks with their structure, real headings and lists, and a short description for each figure. After each conversion it says what changed. This is its summary for the sample document, a 13-page section of OpenStax Physics:

The models make mistakes, so it publishes nothing on its own. A change it isn’t sure of becomes a decision for the instructor, and everything else is accepted and listed so they can look it over. It assists a person and doesn’t certify a page as accessible.

Under the hood, PyMuPDF reads the page layout and Claude reads the equations and describes the figures, with Claude Opus as the main model and Claude Haiku as the cheap one. Each equation it writes is checked against the PDF, and the ones that don’t match are flagged for review.

How I tested it

I wanted a test I couldn’t tune to, so I wrote down the sections, the metrics and the comparisons before any system ran on them. The test set is 15 sections from five OpenStax textbooks (242 pages, 1,369 equations, 509 headings), picked by a seeded rule that excludes every section I used in development. OpenStax publishes these books as web pages with expert-written MathML and alt text, so each section comes with an answer key. I printed each section to a PDF, ran it through the real pipeline and scored the output against the key. The configuration was frozen on the development sample first, and nothing was tuned on these sections.

I compared it with a general chatbot given Purdue’s one-sentence prompt for PDF to accessible HTML (Claude Opus and Claude Haiku, two runs each) and with three open parsers: Docling, MinerU at its basic tier, and PyMuPDF4LLM. Every model call, ReadableSTEM’s included, went to Claude, so against the Opus chatbot the test is my pipeline against a one-sentence prompt on the same model. A few things changed after registration, including two comparisons I didn’t run; PROOF.md lists each one. The model answers are committed, so make proof replays the whole report with no key and no network.

What the results say

The chatbots get more equations right. ReadableSTEM does better on heading levels and figure descriptions.

  • Headings. Its F1 lead over the chatbots is too small for 15 sections to tell from noise. The clear gap is levels: it gets every matched level right, and the chatbots get 0.341 and 0.508.
  • Figures. By the judge, 0 of 138 ReadableSTEM descriptions state something the image contradicts, against 3 of 134 for the Opus chatbot and 12 of 133 for Haiku. The judge is Claude Opus, which hasn’t been checked against people and is also ReadableSTEM’s main model.
  • Cost. The Haiku chatbot is the hard baseline: about an eighth of the cost per page, and 0.600 of equations right. What the extra cost buys is heading levels and figure descriptions.
  • Open parsers. They write no figure descriptions and cost nothing in API calls. On equations ReadableSTEM is well ahead of MinerU, the only one of the three I ran that writes LaTeX (0.504 against 0.174). MinerU’s heading F1 is 0.923, and PyMuPDF4LLM gets every matched level right but finds fewer headings (F1 0.830).

Where it loses

  • Equations end to end. The Opus chatbot finds about as many equations (88.7% against 88.9%) but gets more of the ones it finds exactly right (0.70 against 0.57). With 15 sections the gap is likely, not certain. The scorer is strict too: hand-written LaTeX known to be correct scores 0.70 on a 40-equation sample, so every number reads low.
  • Added symbols. In 8.7% of the equations it writes, it adds a symbol the PDF doesn’t have, against 2% for the Opus chatbot. The chatbot, though, writes many more equations the PDF has no match for (29% against 3%).
  • Chemistry. Equations there are near zero for every system: 0.04 for ReadableSTEM, against 0.61 on Precalculus.
  • Review catches too little. 23.3 of every 100 equations are wrong and nobody is asked about them, about the same as the chatbot, which asks nothing. The equation flags catch 0.34 of the real errors, and the heading flags catch none.
  • Review takes time. It asks for 1.76 decisions per page, against 0.23 on the development sections: about 53 decisions for a 30-page upload.

What the test leaves out

  • The sections are clean OpenStax pages from retired first editions, printed with my own stylesheet. Real course PDFs are harder.
  • OpenStax is public, so the models may have seen these books. I haven’t tested for that, and every Claude arm shares it.
  • I haven’t checked whether a wrong equation changes the meaning, or whether the figure judge agrees with a person.
  • It’s one recording on Claude, with two runs per task.
  • The results are for pipeline version 0.4.0. The code is now at 0.5.0, so they describe the tagged version, not necessarily what runs today.
