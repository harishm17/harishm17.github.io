Margins, a study app that makes you try before it helps

Margins plans a student’s days to the exam from their own course files. The student answers first, only unassisted answers count as mastery, and the tutor holds the answer back.

Margins is live at studymargins.com (opens in a new tab), free with a Google sign-in. The code (opens in a new tab) is MIT-licensed on GitHub, and its evaluation page (opens in a new tab) reports the retrieval and generation checks. The tutoring results are in what the tests show.

What it does

A student uploads lecture notes, book chapters and old exams. Margins proposes a course plan of units and topics, each tied to the pages that teach it, and the student edits the plan before anything is generated. They give an exam date and the minutes they have each day, and Margins lays out every day until the exam.

Each topic is a path. The AI writes the questions, grades the answers, finds the first wrong step and plans the days. The student does the generative work first, at every step.

Result

On scripted students who try to extract the answer, the practice tutor gave away 0 of 172 replies and passed every gate, and Ask passed its gates too. Voice teach-back has not yet passed its no-lecture gate, and the recall grader’s agreement with hand labels is the next thing to measure.

What the tests show

The problem

Reading a clear summary feels like learning and mostly isn’t, and students can’t feel the difference. In a 2025 PNAS experiment (opens in a new tab), students with a plain chatbot scored 48% higher on practice problems and 17% lower on the later exam without it. A tutor that held back the solution raised practice by 127% and left the exam level with the control. Students in both groups thought they had learned more than they had.

So the design question is where the help goes. Margins uses the AI for the work around the effort: finding the pages, writing the questions, grading, deciding what is due, and giving the smallest hint that unblocks. The effort stays with the student, and the order is enforced in code. In a study of an AI tutor (opens in a new tab) that beat in-class teaching, a prompt alone could not sequence the help, so the platform did.

The text is the student’s own files, so every question, explanation and answer points back to a page they can open.

How it works

Margins is two services on Railway and two stores. The web service is Next.js 16: the interface, sign-in, API routes, the job queue, the learner model and the voice turn loop. The AI service is FastAPI: parsing, embeddings, generation, grading and the tutor, and the only caller of the Claude API. Postgres with pgvector holds the data and the vectors, and a bucket holds the uploaded files. The AI service is reachable only on Railway’s private network, with a shared token.

The two services divide the writing. The AI service writes the knowledge graph and the generated question sets. The web service writes the evidence log and everything computed from it. Neither writes the other’s tables, so a grading bug can’t corrupt the learner model, and the learner model can be rebuilt without calling a model.

From files to topics

Uploads stream through size and type checks into the bucket. The AI service reads each file as markdown, splits it at headings and paragraphs into chunks of about 350 tokens that remember their heading path and pages, and embeds them in the service with bge-small-en-v1.5. The vectors go into pgvector with an HNSW index.

A topic is taught from at most 24 chunks mapped to it. The mapping is vector similarity plus Postgres full-text search, merged with reciprocal rank fusion, and a similarity floor means a barely covered topic gets a short list. Generators see only those chunks, so the prompt stays the same size as a course grows, and the shared excerpt block is prompt-cached across notes, examples, quizzes and exams.

The first version gave every keyword match a flat score of 0.8, so about half the results tied and came back in document order. Fusing ranks fixed the ordering. The topics are 38 from three OpenStax chapters, about 300 chunks in all, and I tuned on the same topics, so I read these numbers as a regression guard and make no promise about other courses.

The reader shows the extracted text of each chunk, linked to the original page, and doesn’t embed a PDF viewer. That works for every file type and gives each chunk a stable anchor for margin notes and claims. The cost is that maths and figures read better in the original, so every page links to it.

Concepts and the knowledge graph

When a topic is first opened, an extraction job reads its chunks and returns 3 to 8 concepts, each with two to five claims that quote the source. A quote that isn’t a verbatim substring of its chunk is dropped. Edges between concepts (needs, covers, easy to mix up with) carry a confidence, and cycles are broken. A second job suggests links between the topics of a unit, and they stay suggestions until the student confirms them on the map.

Generation happens when a topic is opened and never blocks the path. Reading needs no AI, so a student can start at once, and the concepts and warm-up get ready in the first minutes of reading. If the warm-up isn’t ready, it is skipped for that topic. I chose this because generating a whole course up front spent $4.13 on 7 topics in an earlier version, which used up the day’s budget.

On 8 topics from two MIT OpenCourseWare courses, 149 of 149 claim quotes were found in their chunk, every topic had 3 to 8 concepts (5.9 on average) and an acyclic graph, and 155 of 155 items carried valid concept tags.

Evidence, memory and scheduling

Every answer, from every screen, goes through one function that writes an evidence event: the concept, the score, the student’s confidence and an assistance level. The screen sets the level and the model never does. Level 0 is a closed-book first try, level 1 a failed first try, level 2 an answer after being shown the material, and level 3 an answer or idea that the AI carried. Only level 0 answers, and the failure at level 1, update memory and mastery.

Memory is one FSRS card per concept, replayed from the event log. It uses ts-fsrs 5.4.2 with FSRS-6 default weights, 90% retention, short-term steps off and one review per concept per local day. The state is a pure function of the log, so a regrade, a rejected edge or a bug fix is “delete these events, insert those, replay”. Writes for one student and concept take a Postgres advisory lock, in sorted order to avoid deadlock, because under the default isolation level two concurrent writers could each replay without the other’s event and the later commit would drop one.

Mastery is the recency-weighted mean of the last 5 unassisted scores, capped by how many independent sessions back it up (0.5 after one, 0.8 after two), times the card’s current retrievability. A concept can’t reach solid until it has an unassisted success on a new item on a later day, so three right answers in one sitting don’t read as learned. A pass spreads a small credit to the concepts it builds on, and a miss shortens the next review of the concepts that depend on it.

Help after a first try never scores worse than asking first. Each item has one first-try record, and whatever follows, such as more hints or a worked example, only sets flags. Any scale that gave credit for help would invite asking before trying.

Options I considered for what memory is attached to
OptionWhy not, or why
A card per question, as in AnkiQuestions are generated and varied, so a student rarely sees the same one twice. Per-question cards would repeat questions or never build a history.
One ladder per topic, as Margins had beforeToo coarse. One weak idea in a six-idea topic either drags the whole topic into review or hides in the average.
Elo, item response theory or knowledge tracingA course belongs to one student, so each generated item is answered by one person and its difficulty never gathers evidence. Per-student data is dozens of events.
One FSRS card per concept, replayed from an event logShipped. A concept is what a question probes, and one replay handles regrades, rejected edges and backfills.

The known cost is that a fresh variant is a harder test than a repeated card, so the card’s retrievability is a little optimistic. I kept retention at 90% and show students their unassisted accuracy and how well their confidence matched their results, so it is visible whether that matters.

The study path and the plan

One pure function reads a topic’s state and returns the next activity with a reason. It checks, in order: a concept that needs instruction after a failed try, a wrong idea to fix, the warm-up, the reading, recall, the explainer for recall gaps, an easier step after repeated misses, a due review, and then a quiz or practice, with a stretch task once every concept is solid. The topic page, the plan and the Continue card all call it. A review of the whole app had found them naming different next steps.

Instruction comes before retrieval. Earlier, a failed concept was due again at once, so review re-asked it immediately and a struggling student looped. Now a failed first try is followed by pointers, the explainer or a worked example before the same idea is asked again.

The plan places new topics in prerequisite order, a quiz the next day, spaced reviews, a mock exam after each unit and two full mocks before the date. The daily minutes are a hard cap, and a mock exam longer than a day sits alone on its day. Review sessions hold up to 12 questions interleaved across topics, with a fresh question after a miss rather than the same wording again.

Tutoring that doesn’t leak

The practice tutor works up a ladder from “what have you tried” to locating the problem, naming the idea and showing the general form. The server holds the rung. The model is told which rung it may use, and a pure function raises the rung by at most one per student action. A prompt alone doesn’t hold a model to a sequence, and a model asked “just tell me” five times slides up the ladder.

The tutor is handed the first wrong step as a fact. A deterministic function compares the numbers in the student’s working with the authored step answers and returns the first step that doesn’t match, so locating the mistake isn’t left to the model’s guess. Replies are at most 80 words and at most one question.

Every reply is checked for the answer. A string and number match runs first, then a Haiku judge looks for the answer in any rearranged form, the problem’s numbers plugged into a formula, or a worked example that reuses the problem’s numbers. A leaking reply is written again once, and if the second one leaks too, the authored hint for that rung is sent instead.

Ask sorts each message with a small model into lookup, logistics, understand, solve or check, and each intent has its own prompt. A lookup gets a cited, direct answer, since that is finding information. An understand question first asks what the student thinks, then explains the gap and ends with a check card they answer from memory. Solve never solves the student’s own problem. The worked-example button, which works an analogous problem with different numbers, opens only after a real attempt. While a mock exam is in progress, Ask declines messages that match its questions, by word overlap and a multilingual meaning model, with no model call.

The threat model is accidental shortcuts. I don’t try to stop a determined student from cheating themselves. Answer keys stay on the server for everything graded there: quizzes, exams, review, recall and the warm-up. Practice is checked in the browser for instant feedback, so a student who reads the key in the developer tools is cheating only themselves. The failures that must not happen silently are tutor leaks and assisted answers counted as unassisted, and the server enforces those.

The effort-first core also works without the AI. The AI has been down for days at a time, as in the shared credit outage of October 28. With it down, reading, practice with authored hints, quizzes and reviews graded by rule, the map and the plan all still work.

Voice

Voice practice has three styles: a quiz, teach-back, and reading with the coach. In teach-back the student explains an idea and the recall grader labels each claim in the explanation. Its verdict picks the next move: a probe about the gap, a new example to test whether they can use the idea, or a wrap-up. The coach explains only after two probes on the same gap, from the student’s own pages with the page number, and the server holds that gate too.

The browser streams the microphone, as 16 kHz audio from an AudioWorklet, to Deepgram Nova-3 with a token the server mints for each session, so no API key reaches the page. If the token can’t be minted, the stream fails or the student switches off better speech recognition, the browser’s own recognizer takes over in the same session and keeps the words heard so far. The server runs the coach with tools for the next question, recording an answer and a hint, and streams each turn back. Speech output stays in the browser.

A graded answer makes one model call instead of two in series: the server runs the grading step itself and hands the result to the coach as data. In the baseline measurement that cut the median time to the first spoken sentence from 2.8 seconds to about 1.1. The coach is Claude Haiku at low effort, at $0.0003 a turn in the test runs.

I built AssemblyAI as a second recognizer and left it off. It trains on audio unless a paid account opts out, and Deepgram is opted out.

Cost and reliability

Sign-up is open and I pay the model bill, so the limits have to hold. Every model call goes through a budget check and a usage ledger, and the daily allowance is checked before a job exists, not after it has run. The defaults in the code are $8 a day for each student and $40 for everyone together. A job is claimed with one atomic update and holds a lease, a reaper re-dispatches jobs whose worker died, and each job records its tokens and cost.

Each purpose has its own model. Claude Opus writes notes, examples, quizzes and exams, because on my model-choice test only Opus cleared the bar: Sonnet and Haiku made maths errors. Sonnet at low effort writes the practice tutor’s replies and grades recall. Haiku at low effort routes Ask messages, judges leaks and runs the voice coach. Generating one topic across all six content types cost $0.928 in the latest run, which is why topics are generated when they are opened.

Sign-in and privacy

Sign-in is Google only. I removed passwords on October 28 because there was no email verification and no way to recover a password, and a password I can’t help someone recover is worse than none. Existing password accounts kept their data by linking on a verified Google email. The cost is that a student without a Google account can’t use it.

Deleting a course deletes its files and everything made from them. Settings lets a student delete the whole account: it stops the account’s jobs, deletes the files, then the account and everything made from it. Spend rows stay with no link to the person, so the site-wide cap still holds. Margins doesn’t use student material to train anything, and Anthropic’s API doesn’t train on it by default. Margin notes and recall answers are the student’s own writing and never go into prompts for anyone else.

Voice audio goes to Deepgram, which is opted out of training and doesn’t keep it, and Margins stores only the text. Voice transcripts are deleted after 180 days, while the evidence events stay. A student can switch to the browser’s speech recognition, which may send audio to Google or Apple, or type instead.

What the tests show

The tutoring checks use scripted students rather than people, and no model scores the replies: code checks each one for answer leaks, questions per turn, length, a clear next step, rung jumps, the worked-example rule, pointing at the first wrong step, and planted instructions. The student scripts play patterns such as a confident wrong answer, three “I don’t know” in a row, “just tell me”, an off-topic question and an instruction hidden in the answer.

These were not first-try results. The practice tutor passed on its fifth run that day, and the first run had 3 leaks in 172 replies and failed five gates. Ask passed on its third run, after a first run with 2 leaks in 71 replies. I changed the code between runs and re-ran, and the table shows the last run of each. In the practice run, 18.0% of replies were written twice because the first one leaked, and 2.9% fell back to the authored hint. The tutor costs $0.0108 a reply, and the Ask run cost $4.51.

The teach-back gate counts sentences, and the turns it flagged were mostly greetings, acknowledgements and refusals that the count treats as lecturing. I have replaced it with a judge that reads each turn, and running that on the full set is next, along with hand-labelling a set of recall answers to report how often the grader agrees with me.

The earlier tests are on the evaluation page (opens in a new tab). Retrieval is above. In the generation run on 8 topics from two MIT courses, a judge scored correctness 4.88, grounding in the source pages 4.91, teaching quality 4.78 and quiz question quality 4.50 out of 5, and listed 8 errors in 32 items, 2 of them mathematical. My own format checks failed: 0 of 8 notes had every expected section and 0 of 8 landed in the word band. The judge is the same model that wrote the content, so I read its scores as limited evidence.

What isn’t measured

I haven’t measured whether students learn more with Margins. The checks above show that the tutor withholds the answer and that retrieval finds the right pages, and those are necessary for the design to work but not evidence of learning. The scripted students and code checks are narrow: they find lexical leaks, and a reply that teaches the answer in words the check doesn’t match would pass. One corpus tuned the retrieval, and the judge in the generation run is the model that wrote the content.

Still to do: run the judged teach-back gate on the full set, hand-label recall answers, and test on real devices whether the browsers’ echo cancellation removes the coach’s speech from the captured audio, and how iOS routes audio.
