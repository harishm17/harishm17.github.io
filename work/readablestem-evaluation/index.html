Evaluating ReadableSTEM against a chatbot and three open parsers

Tests I wrote down before any system ran, against a chatbot given one sentence of instruction and three open parsers. It led on heading levels and figure descriptions and left fewer wrong equations unflagged on papers. It trailed the chatbots on exact equations, and I trace that gap to five causes.

Summary

Claim
Registered and met: on 15 fresh papers my fixes raised exact equations by 6.8 points, and wrong equations with no question to the instructor did not rise. Measured but not registered: on those papers it left clearly fewer wrong equations unflagged than either chatbot (8.4 per 100 against 13.7 and 16.3), and on the textbooks it led on heading levels and figure descriptions. Registered and missed: the claim that it clearly beats the chatbot on equations (it trailed the Opus chatbot on all three sets), and the minimums I set for finding tables.
Sample
15 OpenStax sections (242 pages, 1,369 equations), then two sets of 15 arXiv papers, each fixed before any system ran on it. Handwriting is 24 pages twice, and tables are 7 papers.
Caveat
With 15 sections or papers a set, the intervals are wide. On the textbooks the registered rule called none of its five comparisons in ReadableSTEM’s favour, and the figure judge is a Claude Opus model that I haven’t checked against people.

ReadableSTEM turns STEM course PDFs into accessible web pages. This page reports how it did on tests I registered before any system ran on them, including the ones it didn’t pass. The causes of its loss on equations are further down.

How the tests were run

I wanted a test I couldn’t tune to, so I wrote down the sections, the metrics and the comparisons before any system ran on them. The textbook set is 15 sections from five OpenStax textbooks (242 pages, 1,369 equations, 509 headings), picked by a seeded rule that excludes every section I used in development. OpenStax publishes these books as web pages with expert-written MathML and alt text, so each section comes with an answer key. I printed each section to a PDF, ran it through the real pipeline and scored the output against the key. The configuration was frozen on the development sample first, and nothing was tuned on these sections.

I compared it with a general chatbot given Purdue’s one-sentence prompt for PDF to accessible HTML (Claude Opus and Claude Haiku, two runs each) and with three open parsers: Docling, MinerU at its basic tier, and PyMuPDF4LLM. Every model call, ReadableSTEM’s included, went to Claude, so against the Opus chatbot the test is my pipeline against a one-sentence prompt on the same model. A few things changed after registration, including two comparisons I didn’t run; PROOF.md lists each one. The model answers are committed, so make proof replays the whole report with no key and no network.

For papers I used the same method on arXiv: 15 papers under open licences (71 pages, 1,826 equations), scored against the authors’ own LaTeX, which is closer to a real course PDF than a printed OpenStax page. Later I registered a second set of 15 papers for the fixes described below.

What I registered, and how each test came out

The rule for the textbook set was that a system could be called better only when the 99% interval of the paired difference excluded zero. That interval is wider than the usual 95%, because there were five comparisons with the chatbot. Otherwise the wording is “no difference this set can detect”.

Where it led

Heading levels and figure descriptions were not among the five registered comparisons, so I report them as descriptive. Heading F1 was, and the registered rule called it no difference this set can detect.

  • Headings. Its F1 lead over the chatbots is too small for 15 sections to tell from noise. Among the headings both find, it also gets the level right more often (1.000 against 0.341 and 0.508), which is table stakes for a tool that exists to restore an outline.
  • Figures. By the judge, 0 of 138 ReadableSTEM descriptions state something the image contradicts, against 3 of 134 for the Opus chatbot and 12 of 133 for Haiku. The judge is Claude Opus, which hasn’t been checked against people and is also ReadableSTEM’s main model.
  • Cost. The Haiku chatbot is the hard baseline: about an eighth of the cost per page, and 0.600 of equations right. What the extra cost buys is heading levels and figure descriptions.
  • Open parsers. They write no figure descriptions and cost nothing in API calls. On equations ReadableSTEM is well ahead of MinerU, the only one of the three I ran that writes LaTeX (0.504 against 0.174). MinerU’s heading F1 is 0.923, and PyMuPDF4LLM gets every matched level right but finds fewer headings (F1 0.830).

Where it trailed

  • Equations end to end. The Opus chatbot finds about as many equations (88.7% against 88.9%) but gets more of the ones it finds exactly right (0.70 against 0.57). With 15 sections the gap is likely, not certain. The scorer is strict too: hand-written LaTeX known to be correct scores 0.70 on a 40-equation sample, so every number reads low.
  • Added symbols. In 8.7% of the equations it writes, it adds a symbol the PDF doesn’t have, against 2% for the Opus chatbot. The chatbot, though, writes many more equations the PDF has no match for (29% against 3%).
  • Chemistry. Equations there are near zero for every system: 0.04 for ReadableSTEM, against 0.61 on Precalculus. Most of that turned out to be the score rather than the reading, which the chemistry section explains.
  • Review catches too little. 23.3 of every 100 equations are wrong and nobody is asked about them, about the same as the chatbot, which asks nothing. The equation flags catch 0.34 of the real errors, and the heading flags catch none.
  • Review takes time. It asks for 1.76 decisions per page, against 0.23 on the development sections: about 53 decisions for a 30-page upload.

On real papers the ordering held. On 15 held-out arXiv papers ReadableSTEM got 0.583 of the equations exactly right and both chatbots 0.641, a gap too small for 15 papers to separate. One paper the Opus chatbot refused counts as a zero, as registered; without it the gap is larger. ReadableSTEM cost $0.030 a page, against $0.083 for the Opus chatbot and $0.002 for Haiku. Silent errors

A silent error is a wrong equation that nobody is asked about. A chatbot asks nothing, so all of its wrong equations are silent. ReadableSTEM asks, which is why the number can be lower. On papers it was lower by point estimate, and on the second set the paired difference was clearly lower: 7.9 per 100 below Haiku and 5.2 below Opus.

Why it lost on equations

I traced the gap on equations to its causes, fixed the ones that carried it, and tested on 15 more papers, again registered before any system ran. The rule I set was to find out why from the recorded outputs, which costs nothing, before writing any fix or spending anything on new runs.

Where the points went

I took the 25 papers I developed on and gave every equation that ReadableSTEM or the Haiku chatbot got wrong exactly one cause, the first rule that fit. On these papers ReadableSTEM was 12.4 points behind. Five causes account for it, and the other mechanisms net slightly the other way.

Reading was not the problem. Among the equations it found, its wrong readings were 6.6% of the answer key, against 9.1% for the chatbot. It lost because it didn’t see some equations, or cut them in the wrong place. Three of the causes, on real papers:

Short variables. A letter set in an italic text font looked like text, so a lone S in a sentence was never an equation.

Where a formula stops. The span ran on into whatever sat beside it: the sentence’s parentheses, a unit, a citation.

Captions. A caption was kept as one plain string, so a formula in it was never an equation.

The idea the evidence ruled out

An obvious next step was a repair loop: render the equation the model wrote, compare the picture with the PDF, and retry on a mismatch. Before building it I checked what it could catch. Of the 282 wrong equations nobody is asked about, 1 had the right symbols in the wrong arrangement, the only kind a render-and-compare check could catch that the text-layer check doesn’t already. The rest had other symbols, which the text-layer check already looks at, or a span cut at an edge, where the render matches the cut and the cut is wrong. One in 282 was far below the bar I had set for building it, so I didn’t build it.

I also had a model read each whole page as a second witness for finding equations. It raised the score by 1.7 to 2.5 points on the development papers, but it added 0.6 to 1.1 more silent errors per 100 equations, against a limit of 0.5 that I had set. I dropped it and fixed the same causes from the PDF’s text layer instead.

The fixes and the test

The fixes use the text layer and the position of each glyph, with no change to the prompt, so a recorded model answer replays to the same result. An italic letter between upright words is a variable. A figure’s caption is cut into text and formulas like a paragraph. The parentheses of the sentence, a unit after a number, and a citation leave the formula. A bold letter with an index is a vector.

On the development papers the score went from 0.553 to 0.680, level with Haiku at 0.677. That says little, because I tuned on those papers. So I registered a second test first: 15 new arXiv papers, the rules for success written down, and a limit that silent errors could not rise by more than 0.5 per 100. Results:

  • The fixes were worth +0.068 (interval +0.031 to +0.116), from 0.558 to 0.626.
  • Silent errors did not rise: 9.76 per 100 before, 8.42 after.
  • ReadableSTEM is level with Haiku (0.626 against 0.622) and 5.6 points below Opus (0.682, with an interval that includes zero).
  • The claim I registered, that it clearly beats the chatbot recipe, was not met.

The run cost $13.55 of an $18 cap.

Handwriting

The handwriting set is 24 pages of real human touchscreen ink, 205 expressions from Google’s MathWriting set, registered before the run. ReadableSTEM got 0.566 of the equations exactly right (interval 0.515 to 0.618), so 43% were wrong. None was silent, because the model called most handwritten blocks medium or low confidence and so 5.7 decisions a page were required, against 1.4 on typed pages. It also dropped four whole display equations of one page without a flag, which the tool now catches by comparing the page’s ink with what it read. The cost was $0.066 a page.

By the letter of my rule, an advertising bar of 0.50 and zero silent errors, both held. They did not on the fresh set: 0.589 right on 24 pages (192 expressions), and 2 of 192 wrong without being flagged, both in blocks the model called high confidence. I looked at the wrong ones instead of trying prompts. Of 199 wrong equations over three sets, 65% were ink, or a label, that a reader can’t settle from the page. Another 23% were a notation the label hadn’t used, 8% were misreads of clear ink, and 4% were writing the model left out. A model reading each expression alone from clean ink got 61% to 64% right, the same as reading the whole page. So the accuracy is limited by the writing, not by how I ask.

The model’s confidence told me almost nothing either: it separated right equations from wrong ones barely better than chance (area under the ROC curve, 0.52 to 0.61). That settled the design. I stopped trying to raise accuracy and made the errors visible: on a page read from its image, every equation and every block with a formula in it goes to the instructor. I measured the cost by replaying the recorded answers. Required decisions per page went from 3.58 to 6.44 on scanned typed pages and from 2.00 to 5.69 on scanned papers. Silent wrong equations in those replays are 0 by construction.

Scans of typed pages worked better. Made into scans, 9 of the arXiv papers came out at 0.664 of equations exactly right, against 0.619 for the same papers as typed PDFs, at $0.136 a page.

Tables

In the textbook run tables were off, so a detected table was kept as an image and sent to review. Since then tables are rebuilt as real tables. The layout model proposes ruled, shaded or header-marked grids only, so unruled tables and tables ruled only above and below were never proposed. On five real documents the detector found 14 of 25 tables (recall 0.560). Tracing each miss gave a list of mechanisms: the justified columns, a caption added after the decision, a page of sideways text, a page with more than 30,000 drawn items. The fixes took development recall to 0.920, which again says little.

On a fresh set of seven papers, registered before the run, it found 0.744 of the tables, and 0.914 of what it called a table was one. That missed both floors I had set, 0.85 for recall and 0.95 for precision. The misses there were Roman-numeral captions, bulleted cells and charts boxed by the layout model, none of which the development set had shown. I fixed the causes that run showed, so that set is now a development set, and I don’t have a fresh test of the fixes.

The chemistry score

The near-zero chemistry score looked like a failure to read chemistry. It was mostly something else. The sample was 443 chemistry equations in 12 development documents (79 pages), then 377 equations in five fresh OpenStax Chemistry sections, registered before the run.

What the score compared

The primary score compares the shape of the MathML. OpenStax writes a formula such as H2O(l) with a text run for each group of element symbols and the state symbol as three tokens. My converter, which also built the answer key for the authored document in the first example below, writes one upright letter for each symbol. What I counted

I put each of the 443 equations in one class, from the recorded answers, with no model call. In 244 of them the formula was right and the primary score was wrong only because of how the key spells it: how tokens are packed (75), how scripts are grouped (39), and upright against italic against text letters, bars, numbers and the length of an arrow (130). Counting those, 292 of 443 were right as chemistry. The primary score counted 48.

What it missed were the 94 formulas in table cells, which were off in that run (production now runs with tables on), and the lone × signs, which are not chemistry at all.

The fresh check

I didn’t change the registered score. I added a second one for chemistry only and report both. Then I wrote down predictions and ran the five fresh sections once, with no change afterwards. The primary was 0.135, which I had predicted would fall between 0.05 and 0.20. The chemistry score was 0.552 before the chemistry fixes and 0.560 after, 4.1 times the primary, against a prediction of at least 3.

What the tests leave out

  • The textbook sections are clean OpenStax pages from retired first editions, printed with my own stylesheet. Real course PDFs are harder.
  • OpenStax is public, so the models may have seen these books. I haven’t tested for that, and every Claude arm shares it.
  • I haven’t checked whether a wrong equation changes the meaning, or whether the figure judge agrees with a person.
  • It’s one recording on Claude, with two runs per task on the textbooks and one on the papers, scored by one scorer. Two notation artefacts count against every system by about a point and a half.
  • The textbook results are for pipeline version 0.4.0, the version I registered. The later sets ran on later versions.
  • Every cause was found on my own development papers, and every fix was tuned on them. Only the fresh sets are evidence, and each is 15 papers or fewer.
  • Heading levels are compared with each system’s own top level, and I haven’t checked how much of the chatbots’ low score is that convention and how much is wrong levels.
  • Maths in table cells is still the largest cause I haven’t fixed, along with formulas that wrap across lines.
  • The handwriting is touchscreen ink from one dataset, pasted onto composed pages. It is not paper handwriting.
  • In chemistry, the first edition and the Atoms First edition share text, so I chose topics the development set lacks and did not measure the overlap.
  • The chemistry score is mine, written after the problem was visible. To check it isn’t too generous, 15 pairs of near formulas must stay wrong in its tests, and on the authored document all 100 equations it calls right and the primary calls wrong have the same LaTeX once spacing, text commands and the arrow command are removed.
  • The chemistry score doesn’t put ReadableSTEM ahead of the chatbots. Scored the same way on five development sections, the Haiku chatbot got 49.3% right as chemistry and ReadableSTEM 38.2%, where the chatbot found more formulas in table cells (it missed 42, ReadableSTEM 94). I did not run the chatbots on the fresh sections.

Lessons for your own evals

  1. Write down what “better” means before the run. The differences I care about most, heading levels and figures, are descriptive because I hadn’t registered them.
  2. A claim that is not met stays not met. I registered that it would clearly beat the chatbot recipe. It drew level with one chatbot and trailed the other, so I report it as not met, and the table floors the same way.
  3. Report the cheap baseline. The Haiku chatbot costs about an eighth as much per page and got 0.600 of equations right against 0.504. A comparison with only the expensive chatbot would have hidden it.
  4. Count the errors nobody is asked about, not only the score.
  5. Split the gap by cause before you fix anything. One table of mechanisms, built from outputs I had already recorded and so free, showed that five causes carried the 12.4 points and that reading the maths was not one of them. A score can measure markup rather than meaning, too: before you treat a low score as a failure, put a sample of the “wrong” answers in classes by hand.
  6. Ask what a repair loop could see before you build it. A render-and-compare check could see 1 of the 282 wrong equations nobody is asked about.
  7. When ink can’t be settled, stop tuning and show the errors. Raising accuracy had a ceiling. Sending every handwritten equation to a person did not.
  8. A zero in a small sample can fail to replicate, and numbers from the data you tuned on run high.
