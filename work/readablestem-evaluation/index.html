Evaluating ReadableSTEM with tests registered before they ran

Tests of ReadableSTEM written down before any system ran. On 26 unseen documents it matched a Claude Opus chatbot on equations, let about half as many wrong ones through unflagged, and cost a third as much.

Summary

Claim
  • On 26 documents it had never seen. ReadableSTEM read equations about as well as a Claude Opus chatbot (0.840 right against 0.836), let about half as many wrong equations through with no flag (5.0 per 100 against 9.6), and cost about a third as much ($0.022 a page against $0.068).
  • Registered, claim not made. I had registered five bars for claiming a clearly higher score than that chatbot. Three were missed, and I don’t make that claim: the score was level, the 5.0 wrong equations with no flag were over the bar of 4.0 per 100, and it flagged 0.46 of its wrong equations against a bar of 0.60.
  • Registered and met. Wrong or missing equations with no flag (11.0 per 100 against the chatbot’s 16.4) and a cost under $0.07 a page. In earlier tests my fixes raised the score on new papers without adding wrong equations with no flag.
  • Registered and missed. Chemistry (0.333 right against a bar of 0.80) and finding tables (0.744 found against a bar of 0.85).
Sample
The newest set is 26 documents, 131 pages and 1,715 equations, registered on November 4, 2026. Earlier sets: 15 textbook sections, five sets of 15 to 18 arXiv papers, 24 handwritten pages twice, and 7 papers with tables.
Caveat
Each system ran once, and the newest set can detect a difference of about 3 points. Scoring uses ReadableSTEM’s own speech rules, not a screen reader’s. The newest set has no scans, handwriting or Word files.

ReadableSTEM turns STEM course PDFs into accessible web pages and flags the equations it is unsure of for the instructor to check. This page reports how well that works. Every test on it was registered: I committed the documents, the scores and the bars for success, with a hash, before any system ran on them. The reports and registrations are in the repository (opens in a new tab).

How the tests were run

The protocol for every test on this page
RuleWhat it means here
Written down firstDocuments, scores, comparisons and bars for success are committed and hashed before any system runs. The runner refuses to start if a registered file has changed.
Documents nobody tuned onA seeded rule draws each set and excludes everything used in development. A set I have studied becomes development data.
Answer keys from the sourceThe authors’ own LaTeX for arXiv papers and course documents, and the publisher’s expert-written MathML for OpenStax textbook sections.
A fair baselineA chatbot: Claude Opus given the PDF and Purdue’s one-sentence prompt for turning a PDF into accessible HTML. ReadableSTEM’s model calls go to Claude too, so this is my pipeline against one prompt on the same model.
Intervals and a wording ruleDifferences are paired by document, with a bootstrap interval and a sign-flip test. Unless the interval excludes zero, the wording is “no difference this set can detect”.
Costs measured and cappedEvery system’s cost per page is reported. Each ran once under a spending cap, and the newest run cost $21.98 in all.

At the tag proof-heldout-2026-10, make proof replays the first registered report from the committed model answers, with no key and no network.

The newest set: level on equations, fewer silent errors

The newest set tests the product as it runs today, on 26 documents drawn by rule: 12 arXiv papers, 6 OpenStax sections and 8 course LaTeX documents, 3 of them slide decks. Three chemistry sections registered with it are reported under chemistry.

An equation counts as right when it matches the answer key exactly or when ReadableSTEM’s speech rules read the two in the same words, because a screen-reader user hears speech. An equation the system never found counts as wrong.

Five systems ran: ReadableSTEM at its production settings; a control, the same code with its three newest checks off; the Opus chatbot; the same prompt on the smaller Claude Haiku; and Opus with a longer expert prompt.

ReadableSTEM and the Opus chatbot are level: the paired difference is +0.004, and the set could detect about 3 points. By the wording rule that is no difference this set can detect, and the same holds against the other three systems.

ReadableSTEM cost about a third as much per page as the Opus chatbot. The Haiku chatbot costs far less again and leaves more than twice as many wrong equations with no flag.

Silent errors and what the review screen asks

A silent error is a wrong equation that reaches the page with no flag for the reviewer. A chatbot flags nothing, so all of its wrong equations are silent. ReadableSTEM flags the equations its checks doubt, and each flag is a required decision for the instructor on the review screen. For a tool whose job is to tell an instructor what to check, this is the number I care about most.

ReadableSTEM left about half as many silent errors as the Opus chatbot, and the paired differences from all three chatbots are clear (p 0.006 or smaller). It is also lowest in the second panel, where equations a system never found are counted too.

Flags cost the instructor time: 2.87 required decisions a page. They also caught under half of what was wrong. Flag recall, the share of its wrong equations that ReadableSTEM flagged, was 0.461 against a registered bar of 0.60.

The control shows what the three newest checks add: without them ReadableSTEM leaves 1.57 more silent errors per 100. The interval only just excludes zero and the sign-flip p is 0.06, and I don’t claim that difference.

How the equation score came about

Between the first arXiv set and the newest one I changed the equation pipeline in three rounds. Each started from causes I traced in outputs I had already recorded, which costs nothing, and ended in a registered test on a fresh set.

Where the misses came from

On the 25 papers I developed on, I gave every equation ReadableSTEM got wrong exactly one cause, the first rule that fit. Misreading was a small part: among the equations it found, wrong readings were 6.6% of the answer key. Most misses were equations it never saw or cut in the wrong place, and five causes carried most of them.

The five causes of missed or misplaced equations on the 25 development papers, and the fix for each
CauseWhat happenedThe fix
Short variables in running textA letter set in an italic text font looked like text, so a lone S in a sentence was never an equation.An italic letter between upright words is a variable. A bold letter with an index is a vector.
Where an inline formula starts and endsThe span ran on into whatever sat beside it: the sentence’s parentheses, a unit, a citation or a word.These leave the formula. In a TeX-made PDF the glyphs decide the edges.
Math in table cellsA cell’s math was the table step’s text, not an equation.A cell that is one formula is an equation of its own, shipped on November 3.
Math in captionsA caption was kept as one plain string, so a formula in it was never an equation.A caption is cut into text and formulas like a paragraph.
Bold italic lettersA bold vector was read as plain bold.A letter drawn in a bold italic font is written bold italic.

The ideas the evidence ruled out

An obvious next step was a repair loop: render the equation the model wrote, compare the picture with the PDF, and retry on a mismatch. Before building it I counted what it could catch. Of 282 silent errors, 1 had the right symbols in the wrong arrangement, the only kind a picture comparison catches and the text-layer check doesn’t. I didn’t build it.

The second, whole-page model read tested on the first fresh set raised the score but let more wrong equations through with no flag, and it stayed off. A November version turns a disagreement between the two reads into a required decision. On 12 development papers it changed no reading, a gain of 0.0 points against the 2 I had written as its stop rule, and it stays off too.

The fixes and the tests

The first round fixed the five causes from the PDF’s text layer and glyph positions, with no change to the prompt, so a recorded model answer replays to the same result. On the development papers the score went from 0.553 to 0.680, which says little, because I tuned on those papers. On the second fresh set the fixes were worth 6.8 points (interval +3.1 to +11.6). Column order and a notation convention added 0.6 on the third, or 1.8 when math in table cells counted. Line context added 2.6 on the fourth (interval +1.7 to +3.6).

Three checks came next, each using more of the PDF to catch a wrong reading. Glyph checks compare letter styles with the PDF’s fonts, glyph reconcile corrects a reading where the glyphs leave one answer, and the flag policy flags an accepted equation that looks like past errors. The flag policy was fitted on development papers only. Replayed on the second to fourth fresh sets with its constants fixed and no model call, it took silent errors from 8.83 to 7.77 per 100 and flag recall from 0.29 to 0.38. The newest set is the first held-out run of all three, and they are what its control switches off. I don’t expect the flag policy’s current features to reach the 0.60 bar. The next equation work has to find a signal it doesn’t have yet.

Handwriting goes to review

I registered a bar for advertising handwriting: at least 0.50 of equations exactly right and zero silent errors. The first set was 24 pages of real touchscreen ink, 205 expressions from Google’s MathWriting set. ReadableSTEM got 0.566 right (interval 0.515 to 0.618), and none of the wrong ones was silent. The model called most handwritten blocks medium or low confidence, and those go to review: 5.7 required decisions a page, against 1.4 on typed pages. It did drop four display equations from one page with no flag, which a comparison of the page’s ink with what was read now catches.

On a fresh 24 pages the score held (0.589 right) and the zero did not: 2 of 192 expressions were wrong with no flag, both in blocks the model called high confidence. One was a faithful reading in a different notation, which the score still counts as wrong:

The other was a lookalike:

I read the wrong answers before trying more prompts. Of 199 wrong equations over three sets, 65% were ink, or a label, that a reader can’t settle from the page. A model given each expression alone as clean ink got 61% to 64% right, the same as from the whole page, and its confidence separated right equations from wrong ones barely better than chance (area under the ROC curve, 0.52 to 0.61). The writing limits accuracy, whatever the prompt.

I stopped tuning for accuracy and made the errors visible: on a page read from its image, every equation goes to the instructor. Replaying recorded answers, that raised required decisions a page from 3.58 to 6.44 on scanned typed pages and from 2.00 to 5.69 on scanned papers, with 0 silent errors by construction.

Typed pages that arrive as scans read better than handwriting. Made into scans, 9 of the arXiv papers came out at 0.664 of equations exactly right, level with 0.619 for the same papers as typed PDFs, at $0.136 a page.

Tables: short of the bars I set

I registered two bars for finding tables: recall of 0.85 and precision of 0.95. On a fresh set of seven papers ReadableSTEM found 0.744 of the tables, and 0.914 of what it called a table was one. Both missed.

The detector had started at 14 of 25 tables on five real documents (recall 0.560), because the layout model proposes only ruled, shaded or header-marked grids. Tracing each miss to a mechanism took development recall to 0.920. The fresh set then showed causes the development set hadn’t: Roman-numeral captions, bulleted cells and charts boxed by the layout model. I fixed those too, which makes that set development data. A local set of 45 documents and 197 tables now gives recall and precision of 0.985 and a table-structure score (TEDS) of 0.941. I don’t have a fresh test of these fixes yet.

Chemistry: the score, not the reading

ReadableSTEM’s first chemistry score was near zero, and most of that came from how the score compares formulas. The sample was 443 chemistry equations in 12 development documents, then 377 in five fresh OpenStax Chemistry sections, registered before the run.

What the score compared

The primary score at the time was exact match, which compares the shape of the MathML. OpenStax writes H2O(l) as a text run for each group of element symbols, with the state symbol as three tokens. What I counted

I put each of the 443 equations in one class, from the recorded answers, with no model call. In 244 the formula was right and only the key’s spelling differed: how tokens are packed (75), how subscripts and superscripts are grouped (39), and letter style, bars, numbers or the length of an arrow (130). Counting those, 292 of 443 were right as chemistry. The primary score counted 48.

The fresh check

I didn’t change the registered score. I added a second one for chemistry only, wrote down predictions, and ran the five fresh sections once. The primary came out at 0.135, inside the 0.05 to 0.20 I had predicted. The chemistry score was 0.552, and 0.560 after the chemistry fixes, 4.1 times the primary against a prediction of at least 3 times.

Then I built what the reading needed: a species check on coefficients, charges, states and arrows, and MathML shaped so a speech engine pronounces it. MathCAT, a speech engine, recognized 153 of 153 correct chemistry outputs, up from 136.

The newest set’s chemistry sections

Three OpenStax chemistry sections (123 equations) were registered with the newest set, with bars of their own. None was met. ReadableSTEM got 0.333 right as chemistry (41 of 123) against a bar of 0.80. The bar for the new checks was 5 points above the control, which got the same 0.333: they added nothing here. Silent harm, a wrong chemistry equation with no flag, was 18.70 per 100 (23 equations) against a bar of 1. With 123 equations in 3 sections, a rate near 0.33 has an interval of about plus or minus 0.08.

I report no chatbot comparison for chemistry. Both chatbots wrote their chemistry in mhchem notation, which my registered scorer does not convert, and their scores there say nothing about their chemistry.

What the tests leave out

  • The textbook sections are clean OpenStax pages from retired first editions, printed with my own stylesheet, and real course PDFs are harder. OpenStax is public, so the models may have seen these books. I haven’t tested for that, and every system here shares it.
  • Two chatbots reached spending caps I had set from too low an estimate. I raised the Opus chatbot’s from $10 to $12.50 before I had seen any ReadableSTEM result, and it finished. The strong-prompt chatbot stopped at $9 after 17 of the 26 documents.
  • I haven’t checked whether a wrong equation changes the meaning.
  • I have a written protocol for a VoiceOver test and no results, and none for NVDA or JAWS.
  • The arXiv answer keys follow the authors’ dollar-sign boundaries. Two of the three slide decks come from one repository, and I relaxed the selection rule for decks while the registration was a draft.
  • Every cause was found on my own development papers, and every fix was tuned on them. Only the fresh sets are evidence, and four of them are 15 to 18 papers.
  • The exact scorer is strict: hand-written LaTeX known to be correct scores 0.70 on a 40-equation sample, so exact numbers read low.
  • The handwriting is touchscreen ink from one dataset, pasted onto composed pages. It is not paper handwriting.
  • The table fixes, the LaTeX source path, the long figure descriptions and the Word and EPUB exports have no registered test.
  • The chemistry score is mine, written after the problem was visible. The fresh chemistry sections come from an edition that shares text with the one I developed on, and I did not measure the overlap.
