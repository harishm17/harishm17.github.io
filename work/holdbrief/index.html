Holdbrief: a daily read on every stock you own

A daily brief for each US and Indian stock you hold. Code computes every number and the stance, a language model only explains them, and a checker rejects any sentence the facts don’t support.

Holdbrief is live at holdbrief.com (opens in a new tab). The live demo (opens in a new tab) is read-only and needs no account; sign-in is by invitation. It’s free, and it’s open source under the AGPL: the source is on GitHub (opens in a new tab). The site is my own instance of it.

What it does

A long-term holder doesn’t need a signal to trade. Each day the useful questions are whether anything changed and whether the price is still reasonable for the business. After each market closes, Holdbrief writes a short brief for every stock it covers, 301 US stocks and 200 Indian ones. For each stock it gives:

  • one of three words: Keep, Watch closely or Reconsider;
  • the price against a fair zone, the range in which it calls the price fair;
  • a rating of the business as strong, average or weak;
  • a short written read that cites the facts behind it;
  • what changed since the last close, or a plain statement that nothing did.

Readers who follow stocks also get one email each market morning with only what changed. Everything it publishes is general information and not advice.

Result

The record of every published view starts on 21 October 2026. Its first 60-session grades arrive around January 2027, so I can’t yet say whether the views beat the index.

The problem

Fair-value numbers are easy to distrust. A site prints one figure with no sense of how sure it is, and a language model asked to summarise a stock will confidently type a price that isn’t today’s. I wanted a range that gets wider when the company is hard to value, an honest “not enough to call” when it is, and prose that cannot drift from the numbers beside it.

That decides the structure. Code does everything that can be checked, and the model does only the part that needs language.

How it works

The facts come first. Each stock gets a fact table with one ID per fact. Code ranks the business against its peers, values the price, sets the stance and finds the day’s events. Only then does a model write the short read, from that table and nothing else, and a checker holds every sentence to it.

It’s one FastAPI service and one SQLite database, with the scheduler inside the service. A single process is easy to reason about and easy to restore, and at this size it’s enough.

Data ingestion

Prices and fundamentals come from Yahoo. For US companies the statements also come from SEC company facts, and 8-K item codes become dated events. For India the events come from NSE announcements and its board-meeting schedule, which is the primary source for the next results date. News comes from Finnhub, Exa, Marketaux, NewsData.io and Google News RSS, which is how Indian press gets in. Each item records which provider delivered it, and the page credits it.

The sources fail in different ways, so each news provider runs in parallel with its own timeout, circuit breaker and per-run cap. A quota or a bot block counts as skipped, not failed, and the previous report’s news from the last 7 days carries forward, so one blocked source doesn’t empty a page. Items are matched to the company, which matters when ITC and ITC Hotels share a name. Duplicates are merged across providers, and junk such as law-firm ads is dropped.

All of this data is third-party and can be late or wrong. The report says when something is missing, for example when news was unavailable that day. I took more providers and more code over trusting any one of them.

Ranking the business

Each stock is ranked against the others in its own market, so Indian companies are compared with Indian ones. Peers come from the company’s industry pool, re-ranked by similarity, with a guard against outliers. Three measures make up the rating:

  • quality: profitability, growth and safety, in the style of the quality-minus-junk factor;
  • growth: sales and earnings over three years, and how consistent they were;
  • health: debt, interest cover and liquidity, or the cash runway for a company losing money.

A quality score of 67 or above is strong. Banks aren’t scored on health, and the page says why. Ranking inside a market costs breadth, since 301 and 200 companies are a small pool next to a whole market, but it keeps accounting and capital differences from distorting the comparison.

My own validation tested the quality measure against what stocks actually did, in the US and in India. Over a short window from 2025 to 2026 it came out backwards, with higher-rated stocks doing worse after 60 sessions. The window was short and had survivorship bias, so it’s not a verdict. It does mean the rating hasn’t earned trust, and the how-it-works page says so.

Valuing the price

Each company is routed by type to the methods that suit it: bank, insurer or other asset-light financial, REIT, loss-maker, cyclical, or the default. Most companies get three methods: their own usual valuation range, the range for similar companies, and a discounted cash flow. The cash flow model also gets a simulated range, which is shown but doesn’t change the fair value.

Banks get a justified price-to-book value and a two-stage excess-return model both anchored on book value, because a bank’s cash flows say little about what it earns on its balance sheet. Cost of capital is set per market: a live 10-year yield for the US, and for India the government bond yield less its default spread plus a country risk premium.

The fair value is a weighted median of the methods. A median keeps one extreme method from dragging the result, and a method that is only a floor, or doesn’t fit the company, is shown with zero weight. Analysts’ average target counts for a small share and is labelled as theirs. The weights are my judgement, and the record is too short to test them.

When fewer than two methods are usable, or they’re too far apart, the page says “Not enough to call” and shows the growth the price implies against the growth the company has delivered. I would take a blank over a number I don’t believe.

The fair zone

Every stock gets an uncertainty class from low to extreme, from how much its price swings, how steady its margins are, how much it owes and how far apart the methods are. The class sets the width of the fair zone. It’s about 5% either side of fair value for the most predictable stocks and 15% to 25% for the least, wider where the methods disagree. I chose that over a fixed band because a stable utility and an unprofitable growth company shouldn’t be held to the same tolerance. The estimate around it is at least about 8% either side.

The stance has memory. A price move changes it only after the price holds for 3 closes, and by one step at a time. Fair-value revisions under 3% are ignored, and each move names a reason from a closed list. This makes the view slower to react, which suits someone holding for years and stops it flickering on noise. The whole method is versioned. The current version is 2026.11-f, and each stored report records the version that produced it.

What the model writes, and what checks it

The model decides nothing. It receives the fact table and writes a short read with placeholders such as {{price}} and {{fair_range}}, and the page fills those in with the current values. A read written last week therefore shows today’s price, and the model can’t type a stale one.

A validator then rejects a sentence that:

  • types a figure by hand, or cites a fact that isn’t in the table;
  • gives the wrong sign for its verb, such as “fell” beside a positive number;
  • compares two unlike quantities;
  • uses a tier or band word that contradicts the view.

Units are normalised, so lakh, crore and billions compare correctly. A failing read gets one repair call, and any sentence that still fails is dropped. If the model is unavailable, code writes the read from the same facts. Headlines are marked as third-party text in every prompt, and the company’s identity is anchored in them so a headline about a similarly named company isn’t read as this one.

The model doesn’t compute scores, valuations or fair zones, choose the stance, or invent figures or news. It may propose a one-step stance move, which takes effect only if it cites a real event that is allowed to move the view. The same view goes to every reader.

The checker is strict, and it sometimes rejects a sound sentence. I accept that over a wrong figure, and I loosen its rules when the false rejections show up in the repair counts.

Coverage, caching and cost

Every stock in both markets gets a report every day. Followed stocks are narrated first, and the rest share a capped allowance. The budget is checked before each model call, and a spent budget withholds the prose, never the report: the numbers, stance and fair zone are always published.

The model is called only when something material changed: first coverage, a new stance, a material event or tagged news, a price move of 5% or more, a tracked fact past its tolerance, or a 30-day backstop. Otherwise yesterday’s prose is shown again with today’s numbers. A heavier model handles first coverage, view changes and events, and a lighter one the rest. A refusal or a cut-off moves the call to the next model.

A run makes many calls with the same long system prompt, so it’s cached on the provider’s side and cache reads cost a fraction of normal input. The cost meter prices cache reads and writes, so the caps stay accurate. Together this keeps the daily cost near two dollars for the whole universe.

Scheduling and delivery

Each market runs once after its close, about 21:45 UTC for the US and 10:45 UTC for India, with later passes the same evening and the next morning. A later pass skips any stock already published for the latest session, which makes reruns safe. The runner holds a lock, records every source’s failure rate in a run ledger, and alerts me when an edition is late or degraded.

A published report is never rewritten, so the record can be trusted to show what was said that day. The morning email goes out on weekdays, so Monday’s covers Friday’s session, and its points are written by fixed rules from the same facts.

The record

Every view is stored the day it is published and graded later against the market index and against the median of its sector peers. Keep on a strong business at a fair or cheap price expects outperformance, and Reconsider expects underperformance and is also graded on the drawdown it avoided. Watch closely isn’t graded.

Hit rates carry 95% ranges computed only on independent calls, one per stock per horizon window. Overlapping windows would make the range look far tighter than the evidence allows. Short horizons are noisy, so the longer ones are the ones that count.

I’m not quoting results yet. The record is weeks old, and what would settle the questions I care about, the method weights and whether the wider band helps, is a longer one.

How far it scales

It covers 501 stocks a day on one process and one SQLite file. More markets or readers would move the first limits: the database to a server, the scheduler out of the web service, and a second model provider behind the fallback, which today is another model from the same one. The method itself would need a longer record before I changed the weights or the zone widths.
