Holdbrief: a daily read on the stocks you follow

A daily brief on the US and Indian stocks you follow. Code computes every number and the stance, an LLM writes the explanation from those facts, and a checker removes any sentence it can’t trace back to them.

Holdbrief is live at holdbrief.com (opens in a new tab). The live demo (opens in a new tab) is read-only, covers 30 of the stocks and needs no account; sign-in is by invitation. It’s free and open source under the AGPL: the source is on GitHub (opens in a new tab), and the site is my own instance of it.

What it does

Holdbrief is for people who hold stocks for years. Their daily questions are whether anything changed and whether the price is still reasonable for the business. After each market closes, it writes a short brief on each of the 301 US and 200 Indian stocks it covers. Each brief gives:

  • a stance: Keep, Watch closely or Reconsider;
  • the price against a fair zone, the range in which it calls the price fair;
  • a rating of the business as strong, average or weak;
  • a short written read that cites the facts behind it;
  • what changed since the last close, or a plain statement that nothing did.

Readers choose the stocks they follow and get one email each weekday morning with only what changed; Monday’s covers Friday’s session. A sample brief (opens in a new tab) shows one in full. Every reader sees the same view, and everything it publishes is general information and not advice.

How it works

A site that prints one fair-value figure gives no sense of how sure it is, and a language model asked to summarize a stock will confidently type a price that isn’t today’s. I wanted a range that widens when a company is hard to value, a plain “Not enough to call” when the methods can’t settle on one, and prose that cannot drift from the numbers beside it.

So code computes everything that can be checked: the rating, the fair zone, the stance and the day’s events, all by fixed, versioned rules. An LLM writes the explanation from a table of those facts, and nothing else. A checker rejects any sentence the table doesn’t support, and every published view is stored to be graded later.

What the model writes, and what checks it

The model receives the stock’s fact table, one ID per fact, and writes a short read with placeholders such as {{price}} and {{fair_range}} where the figures go. The page fills them in with current values. A read written last week shows today’s price, and the model has no way to type a stale one.

A checker then rejects any sentence that:

  • types a figure by hand, or cites a fact that isn’t in the table;
  • gives the wrong sign for its verb, such as “fell” beside a positive number;
  • compares two unlike quantities;
  • describes the business or the price in words that contradict its rating or where the price sits against the fair zone.

Units are normalized first, so Indian lakh (100,000) and crore (10 million) compare correctly with billions. A failing read gets one repair call, and any sentence that still fails is dropped. The checker is strict and sometimes rejects a sound sentence. I accept that over a wrong figure, and I loosen a rule when its false rejections show up in the repair counts.

Headlines are third-party text. Every prompt marks them as such, and the writing prompt tells the model never to follow an instruction inside one and names the one company the brief is about.

Code sets every number and the stance. The model has one input beyond the wording: it may suggest moving the stance one step. Code accepts the suggestion only when it cites an event in that day’s data that the rules already mark as able to move the stance, such as earnings that beat or miss expectations by 10% or more.

When the model runs, and what it costs

Every stock gets its numbers, stance and fair zone every day. The model is called only when something material changed: first coverage, a new stance, a material event or news item, a price move of 5% or more, a tracked fact moving past its tolerance, or a 30-day backstop. Otherwise yesterday’s prose is shown again with today’s numbers filled in.

A heavier model handles first coverage, stance changes and events, and a lighter one the rest. Every call carries the same long system prompt, which is cached on the provider’s side, where cache reads cost a fraction of normal input. A budget is checked before each call, with followed stocks first in line and the rest sharing a capped allowance. Together these keep the daily cost near two dollars for all 501 stocks.

What code decides: rating, fair zone and stance

Rating the business

Each stock is scored against peers in its own market, drawn from its industry and re-ranked by similarity, with a guard against outliers. Comparing inside one market keeps differences in accounting and capital rules from distorting the scores, at the cost of breadth: 301 and 200 companies are small pools.

Code computes three scores: quality, growth and financial health. Quality combines profitability, growth and safety, following the quality-minus-junk factor from academic finance. It sets the rating, with health as a guard: 67 or above is strong unless health is weak.

Valuing the price

Each company is routed by type to the valuation methods that suit it. Most get three: its own usual valuation range, the range for similar companies, and a discounted cash flow (the value today of the cash the business is expected to produce). Banks get two methods anchored on book value, the net worth on the balance sheet, because a bank’s cash flows say little about what it earns on that balance sheet. Insurers, property trusts, loss-makers and cyclical companies have their own routes, and discount rates are set per market.

The fair value is a weighted median of the methods, which keeps one extreme method from dragging the result. Analysts’ average target counts for a small share and is labeled as theirs.

When fewer than two methods are usable, or they’re too far apart, the page prints no fair value. It says “Not enough to call” and shows the growth the price implies against the growth the company has delivered.

The fair zone

Every stock gets an uncertainty class from low to extreme, based on how much its price swings, how steady its margins are, how much it owes and how far apart the methods are. The class sets the width of the fair zone: about 5% either side of fair value for the most predictable stocks and 15% to 25% for the least, wider where the methods disagree. A fixed band would hold a stable utility and an unprofitable growth company to the same tolerance.

Setting the stance

One fixed table turns the rating and the price’s position against the fair zone into a stance.

The stance also has memory. A price move changes it only after the price holds for 3 closes, and by one step at a time. Fair-value revisions under 3% are ignored, and each change names a reason from a closed list. A slower view suits someone holding for years and doesn’t flicker on noise. The whole method is versioned (currently 2026.11-f), and each stored report records the version that produced it.

Data and the daily run

Prices and fundamentals come from Yahoo, and US company statements also come from the SEC. Dated events come from the filings US companies make for material events and from the announcements and board-meeting schedule of India’s National Stock Exchange. All of it is third-party and can be late or wrong.

News comes from five providers, because they fail in different ways and I’d rather reconcile several than trust one. Each runs in parallel with its own timeout, circuit breaker and per-run cap. Items are matched to the company, which matters when ITC and ITC Hotels share a name. Duplicates are merged across providers, and junk such as law-firm ads is dropped.

Each market runs once after its close, with retry passes that evening and the next morning. Reruns are safe: a pass skips any stock already published for that session. The runner holds a lock and records every source’s failure rate in a run ledger.

When things go wrong

Failures the design expects, and what a reader sees
FailureWhat happens
A news source is blocked or over quotaIt counts as skipped, not failed. News from the previous report’s last 7 days carries forward, and the page says when news was unavailable
The model errors, refuses or is cut offThe call moves to the next model. If none works, code writes the read from the same facts and the run is marked degraded
The model budget is spentThe numbers, stance and fair zone are still published, and only the written read waits

The record

Every view is stored the day it is published, never rewritten, and graded later against the market index and the median of its sector peers. A Keep on a strong business at a fair or cheap price is expected to outperform. A Reconsider is expected to underperform, and is also graded on the drawdown it avoided. Watch closely isn’t graded.

Each hit rate (the share of graded views that went the expected way) carries a 95% range computed only on independent calls, one per stock per horizon window. Overlapping windows would make the range look far tighter than the evidence allows. Short horizons are noisy, and the longer ones are the ones that count.

Limits

The record is weeks old, and I quote no results from it. A longer one is what would test the two choices I made by judgment: the method weights and the widening fair zone.

The business rating hasn’t earned trust yet. In my own test over a short window from 2025 to 2026, the quality score came out backwards in both markets: higher-rated stocks did worse after 60 sessions. A short window of today’s large companies is too thin for a verdict, and Holdbrief’s how-it-works page says the same.

The whole system runs as one FastAPI service and one SQLite database, with the scheduler inside the service, because a single process is easy to reason about and restore. More markets or readers would move the database to a server, the scheduler out of the web service, and a second model provider behind the fallback, which today is another model from the same provider.

The design notes and validation studies are in the repo’s docs folder.
